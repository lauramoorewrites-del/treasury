> We all have that active, self-directed relationship to the world as young children, through play. But a lot of people lose it when they grow up. Maud’s little sister, Rebecka, who is five, still has it. She can play all day every day. But Maud is losing her drive to play. There was a period during the summer when she was restless and moody in a new way, and it got on our nerves a bit that she’d sit there and complain about being bored without doing anything about it. But then we realized, oh, she doesn’t know how to come up with projects of her own. She doesn’t know that projects are how you play when you’re a grown-up. So Johanna sat her down and talked about it, pointing out how excited Maud had been when she was sewing a bag for her friend’s birthday, but how, when that was finished, she didn’t come up with another project, and then she became moody. You need a project. Maud thought about this for a few days; she really thought about it. Then she came to me, asking me to take her to the library in the island capital so she could look for books about handicrafts and magic tricks.
>
> Seeing how much Maud’s mood has improved since makes me wonder how many adults are out of sorts because they are project-deprived.

— Henrik Karlsson, [“A Project of One’s Own,”](https://www.henrikkarlsson.xyz/p/a-project-of-ones-own) *Escaping Flatland* (2026).
