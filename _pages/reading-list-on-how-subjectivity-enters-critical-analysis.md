Works on where — and how — subjective judgment enters critical and aesthetic evaluation, from the philosophy of taste to contemporary criticism. Grouped by the aspect of the problem each addresses.

## The problem of taste: subjective feeling, universal claim

- David Hume, ["Of the Standard of Taste"](https://www.econlib.org/book-chapters/chapter-part-i-essay-xxiii-of-the-standard-of-taste/) (1757), the fourth essay in *Four Dissertations*. Taste is a sentiment, yet a standard exists: the converging verdict of qualified "true judges" — critics with delicacy, practice, comparison across works, freedom from prejudice, and good sense.
- Immanuel Kant, [*Critique of Judgment*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/48433/48433-h/48433-h.htm) (1790), the "Analytic of the Beautiful" and the "Antinomy of Taste." A pure judgment of taste is subjective — grounded in disinterested pleasure, not concepts — yet lays claim to "subjective universal validity," demanding the agreement of all as if beauty were a property of the object.
- Frank Sibley, "Aesthetic Concepts," [*The Philosophical Review*](https://www.pdcnet.org/phr/content/phr_1959_0068_0004_0421_0450) 68, no. 4 (1959). Aesthetic concepts (graceful, garish, elegant) are not governed by rules or sufficient conditions; no set of descriptive features entails an aesthetic attribution. Applying them requires taste and perception, not the checking of criteria.

## What a critical judgment claims: faultless disagreement

A judgment of taste is asserted as if it were true — it demands agreement, and to disagree is to be wrong — yet it also seems that in matters of taste no one need be at fault. These works analyze that double structure, which is where the critic's private response and the public claim it makes come apart.

- Mary Mothersill, [*Beauty Restored*](https://bookshop.org/search?q=9780198249603) (Clarendon Press, 1984). There are no principles or laws of taste, yet judgments of taste are genuine, truth-apt judgments about particular objects — a form of aesthetic realism compatible with the absence of general rules.
- Max Kölbel, "Faultless Disagreement," [*Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*](https://academic.oup.com/aristotelian/article-abstract/104/1/53/1791377) 104, no. 1 (2004): 53–73. Coins the term for disputes — paradigmatically about taste — in which two parties hold contradictory views yet neither has made any mistake.
- Peter Lasersohn, "Context Dependence, Disagreement, and Predicates of Personal Taste," [*Linguistics and Philosophy*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10988-005-0596-x) 28, no. 6 (2005): 643–686. The truth value of a taste sentence ("tasty," "fun") is relativized to a "judge," so speakers can genuinely disagree without either being objectively wrong.
- John MacFarlane, [*Assessment Sensitivity: Relative Truth and Its Applications*](https://bookshop.org/search?q=9780198776819) (Oxford University Press, 2014). A relativist semantics on which the truth of a taste or knowledge claim depends on a context of assessment — a systematic alternative to treating such judgments as flatly objective or merely contextual.
- Crispin Wright, "Intuitionism, Realism, Relativism and Rhubarb," in [*Truth and Realism*](https://academic.oup.com/book/32897/chapter-abstract/276568955), ed. Patrick Greenough and Michael P. Lynch (Oxford University Press, 2006), 38–60. Resists the inference from faultless disagreement to relativism about taste, arguing the phenomenon can be accommodated without making taste judgments assessment-relative.

## How a critic's judgment actually operates

- Arnold Isenberg, "Critical Communication," [*The Philosophical Review*](https://www.pdcnet.org/phr/content/phr_1949_0058_0004_0330_0344) 58, no. 4 (1949). A critic's reasons do not entail the verdict; they function as "directions for perceiving," singling out features and inducing a "sameness of vision" so the reader comes to see in the work what the critic sees. Criticism persuades by directing perception, not by proof.
- Stanley Cavell, "Aesthetic Problems of Modern Philosophy" (1965), collected in [*Must We Mean What We Say?*](https://bookshop.org/search?q=must+we+mean+what+we+say+cavell) (Cambridge University Press, 1969). Following Kant, the aesthetic judgment claims to speak in a "universal voice" — a claim on others' agreement made without rules or proof. See also "A Matter of Meaning It" in the same volume.
- I. A. Richards, [*Practical Criticism: A Study of Literary Judgment*](https://bookshop.org/search?q=practical+criticism+i+a+richards) (1929). Richards gave roughly 400 Cambridge students thirteen poems stripped of author and date, then catalogued their written "protocols." The exercise documented systematic misreading — stock responses, sentimentality, preconceived opinion — as evidence of how subjective and extraneous factors distort literary judgment.

## Subjectivity as socially and institutionally produced

- Pierre Bourdieu, [*Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste*](https://bookshop.org/p/books/distinction-a-social-critique-of-the-judgement-of-taste-pierre-bourdieu/6722189) (1979; English translation by Richard Nice, Harvard University Press, 1984). Taste is not pure or individual but socially produced: it tracks class position and "habitus," and functions to reproduce social hierarchy. Disinterested judgment is itself a class disposition.
- Stanley Fish, [*Is There a Text in This Class? The Authority of Interpretive Communities*](https://bookshop.org/p/books/is-there-a-text-in-this-class-the-authority-of-interpretive-communities-stanley-fish/6725083) (Harvard University Press, 1980). Meaning is neither resident in the text nor freely created by an isolated reader; it is produced by "interpretive communities" whose shared assumptions determine what a text can mean.
- Barbara Herrnstein Smith, [*Contingencies of Value: Alternative Perspectives for Critical Theory*](https://bookshop.org/search?q=9780674167865) (Harvard University Press, 1988). Literary and aesthetic value is neither intrinsic to the object nor merely "in the eye of the beholder" but a contingent effect of shifting relations among objects, subjects, and circumstances — continuously produced by evaluative practice, with no stable objective standard.
- Roland Barthes, [*Criticism and Truth*](https://bookshop.org/search?q=9780816614813) (*Critique et vérité*, 1966; English translation by Katrine Pilcher Keuneman, University of Minnesota Press, 1987). A reply to Raymond Picard, who had attacked the "nouvelle critique" for lacking objectivity. Barthes argues that criticism is a form of writing that avows its own language and subjectivity, and that the demand for a single objective meaning is untenable.

## The status of the value judgment

- Northrop Frye, [*Anatomy of Criticism: Four Essays*](https://bookshop.org/p/books/anatomy-of-criticism-four-essays-northrop-frye/10424454) (Princeton University Press, 1957). The "Polemical Introduction" argues criticism should be a systematic, autonomous body of knowledge modeled on a science. Frye separates criticism-as-knowledge from value-judgment — which he treats as subjective, historically transient, and unreliable — and excludes the latter from criticism proper.
- Michael W. Clune, [*A Defense of Judgment*](https://bookshop.org/p/books/a-defense-of-judgment-michael-w-clune/ee5e6da30810bf85) (University of Chicago Press, 2021). Argues that literary studies has wrongly disavowed judgments of artistic value as prejudice or status-marking, and defends aesthetic judgment and critical authority — grounded in cultivated perception — against egalitarian relativism.

## The subjectivity concession as a deniability move

Conceding that criticism is subjective can do rhetorical work: like "I was only joking," "of course it's subjective" can cancel commitment after the fact and cast anyone who took the judgment at its word as naive. These works theorize the two halves of that maneuver — the appeal to personal opinion and the joke defense — though none has yet been applied to the practice of literary criticism.

- Ida Vikøren Andersen, "'Well, That's Just My Opinion': The Principle of Expression and the Public Debate," [*Journal of Deliberative Democracy*](https://delibdemjournal.org/article/id/958/) 18, no. 1 (2022). "That's just my opinion" reframes a contestable claim as an inviolable personal expression, functioning as an "argument-repellent" that evades reason-giving and discourages counter-argument.
- Zoe Walker, "Just Kidding? Two Roles for the Concept of Joking in Political Speech," [*The Philosophical Quarterly*](https://academic.oup.com/pq/article/74/4/1338/7502969) 74, no. 4 (2024): 1338–1357. Calling an utterance "just a joke" gives the speaker "aesthetic deniability" — deniability "on the cheap" — because the joke's aesthetic dimension both removes the grounds for reading it as sincere and lets the speaker perform "aesthetic gaslighting," making a hearer feel humorless for having taken it straight.
- Elisabeth Camp, "Insinuation, Common Ground, and the Conversational Record," in [*New Work on Speech Acts*](https://academic.oup.com/book/9256/chapter/155949220), ed. Daniel Fogal, Daniel W. Harris, and Matt Moss (Oxford University Press, 2018), 40–66. Insinuation conveys risky content without entering it into the "conversational record" of public commitments, which is what preserves the speaker's deniability.
- Elisabeth Camp, "Just Saying, Just Kidding: Liability for Accountability-Avoiding Speech in Ordinary Conversation, Politics and Law," in [*From Lying to Perjury*](https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110733730-010), ed. Laurence R. Horn (De Gruyter Mouton, 2022), 227–258. Joking and insinuation shield speakers from accountability, but not absolutely: liability turns on how a reasonable hearer should be expected to interpret the utterance, not on the speaker's claimed intent.
- Jennifer Saul, [*Dogwhistles and Figleaves: How Manipulative Language Spreads Racism and Falsehood*](https://bookshop.org/search?q=9780192871756) (Oxford University Press, 2024). "Figleaves" are bits of language that attenuate a speaker's apparent commitment to an unacceptable claim, making it more acceptable while providing deniability.

## The critic on her own subjectivity

- Lauren Oyler, [*No Judgment: Essays*](https://bookshop.org/search?q=9780063235359) (HarperOne, 2024), especially "My Perfect Opinions." A practicing critic's first-person account of critical conviction: the position that an opinion in criticism is a reasoned conclusion one takes to be correct, not "just an opinion." (Published in the UK by Virago as *No Judgement: On Being Critical*.)
- Ann Manov, ["Star Struck,"](https://www.bookforum.com/print/3004/lauren-oyler-s-meditations-on-goodreads-anxiety-and-gossip-25333) *Bookforum* (Spring 2024). The review of *No Judgment* — a takedown that itself proceeds from the presumption of correctness it examines in its subject.
