> It is perfectly true that she obsessed me, in spite of the fact that she died when I was thirteen, until I was forty-four. Then one day walking round Tavistock Square I made up, as I sometimes make up my books, To the Lighthouse; in a great, apparently involuntary, rush. One thing burst into another. Blowing bubbles out of a pipe gives the feeling of the rapid crowd of ideas and scenes which blew out of my mind, so that my lips seemed syllabling of their own accord as I walked. What blew the bubbles? Why then? I have no notion. But I wrote the book very quickly; and when it was written, I ceased to be obsessed by my mother. I no longer hear her voice; I do not see her.
>
> I suppose that I did for myself what psycho-analysts do for their patients. I expressed some very long felt and deeply felt emotion. And in expressing it I explained it and then laid it to rest. But what is the meaning of "explained" it? Why, because I described her and my feeling for her in that book, should my vision of her and my feeling for her become so much dimmer and weaker? Perhaps one of these days I shall hit on the reason; and if so, I will give it . . .

— Virginia Woolf, "A Sketch of the Past," collected in [*Moments of Being*](https://bookshop.org/search?q=9780712646185) (Hogarth Press, 1976).

> A couple of years later, I was working on a book which I didn't know how to categorise. If I had to call it something, I would have been forced to call it memoir, even though I was uncomfortable with that. As with A Line Drawn, the book followed my life in Madrid, but with some additions, some moving around of the events in the narrative and so on and so forth. Then I remembered Pain and Glory and Almodóvar's quote and started calling the book project autofiction, thinking of my "self-fiction" translation and feeling that was probably what it was: a (partly) fictionalised account of self.
>
> That project remained as autofiction and was temporarily put on hold after I was asked to put together A Line Drawn. My intention was always to return to it, which I dutifully did once the story and essay collection was done, dusted, and out in the world. It was a great surprise to me to find that I had unwittingly nullified the autofiction project by working on A Line Drawn: I found I had said everything I wanted to say about Madrid, identity, journeying, and my experiences.

— Jayne Marshall, interviewed by Laura Moore, ["Writing That's Intimate but Not Necessarily Autobiographical,"](https://www.strangeclarity.com/p/writing-thats-intimate-but-not-necessarily) *Strange Clarity* (2026).
