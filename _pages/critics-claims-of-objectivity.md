Polymath David Bentley Hart brings us the claim that whether (Cormac McCarthy’s) writing has the property of being revolting is an objective matter:

> The most lauded of his early books, *Blood Meridian*, is full of such prose, **much of which, objectively considered, is revoltingly inept**; maybe he gets away with it there, but only because the whole is such a mad, sanguinary, and opulently grotesque extravaganza that nothing in its contents or structure feels more excessive than anything else; everything is in proportion precisely because all of it is equally turgid and hyperbolic.

— David Bentley Hart, [“Mr Toad and the Judge,”](https://davidbentleyhart.substack.com/p/mr-toad-and-the-judge) *Leaves in the Wind* (2023). Emphasis mine.

Viewing Hart’s “Mr Toad” piece as a whole, though, he mostly disclaims objectivity—which makes the bolded claim above so jarring. For instance, he says the following, citing Kant:

> Inevitably, any confession of one’s tastes in the arts will at various points run afoul of someone else’s. The reason for this can be found in Kant’s *Critique of Judgment*: though our aesthetic judgments are individual verdicts based on private rules about expressions of the free play of imagination, they can of their nature be pronounced only as if they were universal deliverances of reason. This being so, I offer the following remarks not as any kind of refutation, much less as reproach, but simply as my own lonely “Here I stand, for it were not safe to move.”

That said, I disagree with Hart *and* Kant on this point: “they can of their nature be pronounced only as if they were universal deliverances of reason.” But now is neither the time nor the place for me to take on Kant.

This reflection by Hart on what he sees in McCarthy’s work as “the close confines of familiar prison walls” stands, to me, as an allegory for the prison of subjectivity we none of us can escape:

> Having said all this—and having withheld a good deal more in the same vein—I know that many persons I respect (you and R— among them) hold McCarthy in much higher regard than I do. And, when I remember the genuinely impressive moments in his work, I have to acknowledge that I am as purblind as anyone else when it comes to matters of personal taste, and must grant that I may be missing something very obvious or, at any rate, very significant. I also cannot help but notice, in casting an eye back over what I have just written, that I repeatedly use the word “American” in an obviously opprobrious manner. This suggests to me that I may be prey to some deeper psychological motive that makes me insensible to things that others seem to have no difficulty perceiving. What to them look like spacious prospects upon a strange new world look to me like the close confines of familiar prison walls.

It’s an eloquently expressed passage on our innate subjectivity.

I couldn’t resist commenting publicly on his piece, especially given his treatment of McCarthy’s aversion for Proust and James, a nugget I’d excavated in an unpublished essay. Here’s an excerpt from my [comment](https://substack.com/@strangeclarity/note/c-374436245?r=emcf2&utm_source=notes-share-action&utm_medium=web) on 30 October 2026:

> I read this from a metacritical perspective with great interest. You cite Kant, and frame your discussion as a “confession” of your taste. Turning to McCarthy, you warn that “I am as likely as not to mistake my own personal distaste for an objective critical judgment made in good faith.” All the same, you mention that much of *Blood Meridian*, “objectively considered, is revoltingly inept.” Was that a purposeful slip, meant ironically, or did you genuinely mean to claim that objective consideration necessitates a “revoltingly inept” verdict (for much of the book)? 
>
> By the way, I disagree with you here: “He may not have liked Proust or James—we are back to that aforementioned notorious interview here—but he made a colossal fool of himself when he said, ‘I don’t understand them. To me, that’s not literature.’”
>
> I don’t read it as buffoonish. Even granting that he was measuring them against his “issues of life and death” standard, I read it as a candid admission of subjectivity. He’s not saying that Proust and James aren’t literature… full stop. He’s saying they don’t move him, and he impliedly recognizes that he’s missing something that others see – “I don’t understand them.” In fact, I take it as a close cousin to your statement here: “I have to acknowledge that I am as purblind as anyone else when it comes to matters of personal taste, and must grant that I may be missing something very obvious or, at any rate, very significant.” *He* doesn’t get Proust and James, and so *to him* they’re not literature – i.e., they don’t figure in his personal canon.
