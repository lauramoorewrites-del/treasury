I tend to be suspicious of self-help sounding content, especially when it appears wrapped with a bow. I don’t know what that suspicion is all about, but it was biasing me against this Substack post “[How to make something when you want to make everything](https://eceaybikeala.substack.com/p/how-to-make-something-when-you-want)” and its parable of the apple tree. Ece, the author, was feeling overwhelmed by a slew of incoming ideas that she couldn’t keep up with. She looked at the apple tree in her backyard and the fallen apples rotting beneath it. And she realized: I don’t need to hold onto all these rotten apples in my backyard. I don’t need them. More apples will come. The same was true for her ideas.

Despite my visceral resistance to the parable (is it that the apple metaphor is too cute?), the post ultimately won me over. Because this metaphor does indeed line up with my personal experience.

I’ve battled with the problem of too many apples myself, repeatedly. From a 12 September 2026 journal entry:

> I’m coming up with far more essay ideas than I could possibly have time for, and I think that’s part of the overwhelm. I was telling someone about this. Writing is my hobby; it is an obligation I place only on myself. So any urgency is self-imposed, and there’s no reason why time can’t stretch to as long as it takes... And yet I feel time pressure. Why is that? I think it’s because new ideas keep coming and building pressure, even as the completion pace slows down, the ideation pace doesn’t.

— Laura Cecile Moore, journal entry, 12 September 2026.

If you spend all your time trying to preserve those apples (or worse yet, sitting mired in paralysis over them), you’re not going to be doing anything else. A little pressure can be good, a lot of pressure can be counterproductive.

Yet the metaphor’s power doesn’t stop with the rotten apples. The author *really* got me with her deepening turn (“making the oatmeal”):

> I wanted to make everything, yes. But even when I picked one idea, I tried to make everything out of it, too. Choosing to turn an apple into anything less than a grand dessert always felt a little sad to me. Wasteful, even. So I spent months protecting my ideas from becoming smaller than their full potential and, in doing so, protected them from becoming anything at all.

— Ece Aybike Ala-Pettersen, [“How to make something when you want to make everything,”](https://eceaybikeala.substack.com/p/how-to-make-something-when-you-want) *ece’s little corner on the internet* (2026).

I’ve encountered a version of this idea expressed in the writing advice “don’t save your ideas, spend them.” If you have an insight, don’t wait for the perfect opportunity that may never come. Spend it now; you’ll always get more. I tell myself that every so often when I realize I’m hoarding ideas. (The reminder doesn’t always work.)

Embedded in the apple tree post, however, is another tidy metaphor I really dislike. It’s from Elizabeth Gilbert’s book *Big Magic*, and it was related to me secondhand (and it was enough to turn me off the book forever). Here’s how Ece’s post tells it:

> Gilbert pictures ideas as these little bodiless bits of energy roaming around the world, looking for someone who might be willing to bring them to life. One finds you, you feel that little spark of *“Ooh, I want to make this!”* and for a while the two of you belong to each other.

— Ece Aybike Ala-Pettersen, [“How to make something when you want to make everything,”](https://eceaybikeala.substack.com/p/how-to-make-something-when-you-want) *ece’s little corner on the internet* (2026).

While it’s true that ideas necessarily build on the work of others, and I buy into some notion of collective cultural heritage, I don’t like Gilbert’s model — at all. It undersells the role of the individual, and by extension, their uniqueness. It comes close to treating humans as fungible, an idea I’m viscerally opposed to. The fact that a few humans might land on the same idea doesn’t mean that it wasn’t still original to them. And, the execution of any idea will necessarily be unique to the person executing it. We are each fated to our own perspective, but those restraints are also beautiful. Even if the shift in degree is vanishingly small, no two people see things precisely the same way.
