In Isenberg’s [“Critical Communication,”](/treasury/critical-communication-by-arnold-isenberg/) he says that reading criticism without the work in view is “a blank and senseless employment.” That’s because the criticism only carries meaning when considered in the work’s context. He is talking about art criticism, when it’s easier to hold the entire work in mind. Literary criticism, and book reviewing, has an altogether different posture. Particularly book reviews, which discuss works that the review reader has usually not read, and often doesn’t plan to. Wrapped up in this, I think, is the critic’s obligation to describe a work faithfully, which is even more obligatory for books that are reviewed. Many critical reviews, especially those of the takedown variety, breach this ethical obligation by parading a series of faults, which compress and skew the original work, in deceptive fashion.

> Peck will seize upon a sentence and pick it apart; about one in “The Black Veil,” he writes: It’s “just wrong, grammatically, stylistically and, most importantly, semantically. It just doesn’t make sense.” The awful thing is that he is often right. By the time you have read a few thousand words of Peck’s sarcastic, belligerent (and witty) evisceration of Moody, you forget that the poor writer has in fact written a haunting portrait of his Mayflower family and its tormented past. Even if it’s not Moody’s best effort, does this merit the fury that Peck rains down upon it?

— James Atlas, [“The Takedown Artist,”](https://www.nytimes.com/2003/10/26/magazine/the-takedown-artist.html) *The New York Times Magazine* (2003).
