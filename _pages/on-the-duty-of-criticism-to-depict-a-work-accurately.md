In [“Critical Communication,”](/treasury/critical-communication-by-arnold-isenberg/) Arnold Isenberg says that reading criticism without the work in view is “a blank and senseless employment.” Criticism only carries meaning when considered in the work’s context, which must be immediately accessible. Isenberg is specifically talking about art criticism, but his logic would extend naturally to other kinds. Yet heeding his rule is more difficult when it comes to works that take more time to consume, which is especially true for books. 

Further, literary criticism in general, and book reviews in particular, are often read by someone who never read the work in question, and doesn’t plan to. Heeding Isenberg’s logic, the book critic or reviewer has an especial obligation to describe the book accurately. Yet many negative reviews breach this ethical obligation by parading a series of faults, which compress and skew the original work, in deceptive fashion.

This is a major fault of Renata Adler’s takedown of Pauline Kael’s review collection, where Adler’s paragraphs are stuffed full of out-of-context snippets from Kael. See Renata Adler, [“The Perils of Pauline,”](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1980/08/14/the-perils-of-pauline/) *The New York Review of Books* (1980), on Kael’s *When the Lights Go Down*.

A related demonstration, from James Atlas’s profile of Dale Peck, [“The Takedown Artist”](https://www.nytimes.com/2003/10/26/magazine/the-takedown-artist.html):

> Peck will seize upon a sentence and pick it apart; about one in “The Black Veil,” he writes: It’s “just wrong, grammatically, stylistically and, most importantly, semantically. It just doesn’t make sense.” The awful thing is that he is often right. By the time you have read a few thousand words of Peck’s sarcastic, belligerent (and witty) evisceration of Moody, you forget that the poor writer has in fact written a haunting portrait of his Mayflower family and its tormented past. Even if it’s not Moody’s best effort, does this merit the fury that Peck rains down upon it?

— James Atlas, [“The Takedown Artist,”](https://www.nytimes.com/2003/10/26/magazine/the-takedown-artist.html) *The New York Times Magazine* (2003).
