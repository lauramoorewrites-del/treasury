I like Quentin Bell’s turns of phrase in the book *Charleston: A Bloomsbury House & Garden*. Like:

> It is built in the vernacular style of the region; it is a decent and sensible place without any ghosts. To be sure there were places where, on a dark winter’s night, a child taking his candle upstairs to bed might walk hurriedly past dark entries and hum a military tune, but there was nothing terrifying as Asheham could be terrifying even in broad daylight.

— Quentin Bell and Virginia Nicholson, [*Charleston: A Bloomsbury House and Garden*](https://archive.org/details/charlestonblooms0000bell), photographs by Alen MacWeeney (Frances Lincoln, 1997).

Bell started writing the book, which features photographs by Alen MacWeeney, when he was 85. As he lost strength, Bell asked his daughter, Virginia Nicholson, to help him. After he died with the manuscript unfinished, his publisher and Nicholson’s mother urged her to carry the project forward. Given the personal nature of the writing, each written segment has a byline noting whether the author was Bell or Nicholson.

Bell gives an account of the preservation of Charleston that spotlights all the personalities that made it so. When the doors were ready to be opened to the public, they expected about a half-dozen visitors a week. Instead, visitors came “not single… but in battalions.”

> Charleston, which began as the refuge of a small band of highly unpopular refugees and developed as a centre for the small society of Bloomsbury, has become a popular institution, a kind of time capsule in which the public can examine a world which has vanished.

I’ve also been reading *The Writer’s Room: The Hidden Worlds That Shape the Books We Love*, by Katie da Cunha Lewin. The author opens with a visit to Virginia Woolf’s country home at Rodmell, called Monk’s House. The book’s premise is that the spaces in which writers worked hold particular interest to the rest of us, but I think this phenomenon is not limited to writers. What’s central is the “time capsule” quality Bell mentioned, wherein a particular place is preserved as it was in a particular time. The place must hold some draw: it’s connected to the aura of a specific figure or milieu, or a past way of living that’s now lost. Its preservation offers a portal through liminal spaces that are usually closed off.

## Charleston history

The book is supposed to be a photographic account of Charleston, but Quentin Bell’s text brings the photos to life. The house was leased by Vanessa Bell in 1916, to give her life partner Duncan Grant and his romantic partner David Garnett (it was complicated) a farming dispensation from war service. She brought Julian and Quentin (aged nine and six) and a small staff. As Quentin Bell puts it, “by that time Clive’s marriage to Vanessa was one in name only,” and his father was living elsewhere. During Christmas 1918, Angelica Bell was born to Vanessa at Charleston. Angelica was generally believed to be Clive’s daughter (presumably with the exception of Vanessa, Clive, and Duncan), but she was in fact Duncan’s, making her the half-sibling of Julian and Quentin.

After the war, the family used it as a summer home. That changed with Julian’s death in 1937 in the Spanish Civil War and the onset of World War Two, when Clive Bell, Vanessa, and Duncan Grant moved in full time. Vanessa and Clive lived there until they each died, in 1961 and 1964. Grant moved out shortly before his death, in 1978 at age 93.

I dug up a 1981 New York Times article about the house’s preservation. Either for lack of true facts or to avoid scandalous matters, the article says that the house was Vanessa and Clive’s, and that “the artist Duncan Grant” “had lived at Charleston with the Bells from the beginning.” Not exactly. Dorothy Parker famously quipped of the Bloomsbury milieu that “they lived in squares, painted in circles, and loved in triangles.”

## Life at Charleston

Writing of what is preserved to posterity as Clive Bell’s study, Quentin Bell takes us back to how it was used in the family’s first years at the house, when it was a living room:

> The fire in this room because a sort of kiln when we children played there. We had found a nice vein of blue clay near the pond and this could be modelled into bricks, pillars and domes and buried in the fire. Here it would either explode or emerge hardened and changed to a lovely salmon pink. With these it was possible to build houses, streets, temples, palaces and, of course, fortifications. The floor of the room would be transformed into a theatre of war.

![Clive Bell’s study at Charleston: a fireplace surround painted with grey and lilac circles, green distempered walls, a still life above the mantel, and an inlaid table in the foreground]({{ "/assets/images/charleston-house-86b330aa.jpg" | relative_url }})

The fireplace in the old family room, now preserved as Clive Bell’s study. Vanessa painted the fireplace circle motifs in 1925 to 1926. The unevenly painted green walls were done with distemper by Nerissa Garnett under Duncan Grant’s supervision in 1971. Distemper is a bit of an archaicism, referring to a water-based paint that produced this uneven effect. It was a favorite of Vanessa’s and Duncan’s. It reminds me of the softly varied backgrounds in paintings, which avoid flat applications of color because they read as unrealistic to the eye.

Distemper is a niche product. It was traditionally made with animal glue as the binder for colored chalks and pigments. Modern versions by Farrow & Ball and Little Greene that mimic the effect are made sans animal collagen.

In a coda to her father’s reflections on Clive Bell’s study, Virginia Nicholson adds:

> [Clive] seems not to have much enjoyed the process of writing, being a perfectionist who in a curious way wanted to write without having to bother about writing: it did not come naturally to him.
